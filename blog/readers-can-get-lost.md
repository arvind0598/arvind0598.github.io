Hypertext research has long reported disorientation and cognitive overhead. Conklin's 1987 survey coined both terms. The extra decisions about where to go next compete with understanding what you're reading.

A [review of reading hypertext](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/200304b3639d5ac8382419099efda8a22b822bd1) (DeStefano and LeFevre, 2007) found that readers with low prior knowledge or low working memory were disadvantaged. Hierarchical structure helped. A [2023 experiment](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0001691823000124) with 90 readers found more "mind wandering" with a networked link structure than a hierarchical one.

The same work suggests fixes. In [one study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563208001258), link suggestions raised inference scores (4.52 vs 3.33). Three links versus eight made no difference to learning. Conklin and Begeman found that browsing primary links first, with an overview map, made networks easier for casual readers. Conklin's survey also describes Bush's trails and a default reading path.

This structure loosely models what "Becuase" attempts to do too. The entry post is essentially an overview, and the remaining questions form the hierarchy. The questions are the link suggestions. We do not have any definition of a "primary path" though, and I can't think of a rubric to define one.

The remaining risk is the audience most likely to get lost: novices. For them, a linear article may simply be the better choice. That fits the claim that Because is an alternative to articles, not a replacement. The auto-generated long form version should solve for this.
